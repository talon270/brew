# What "specialty coffee" actually means

Strip away the marketing and it means two things: the coffee was grown and processed well enough to taste of somewhere in particular, and somebody roasted it to show that off rather than to hide it.

That's it. It isn't about latte art, exposed brick, or paying ₹450 for a cup.

## The practical difference

Commodity coffee is blended from many farms, roasted dark, and sold without a date. It's built to taste the same every time, which means it's built to taste of roast — smoke, char, bitterness — because roast is the one thing you can control when you don't know what's in the bag.

Specialty coffee is traceable. The bag tells you the estate, the altitude, the processing method, and crucially **when it was roasted**. Roast it lighter and the differences between origins survive: an Ethiopian tastes of blueberry and jasmine, a Karnataka washed lot tastes of chocolate and orange, and neither tastes like the other.

## What to expect the first time

Two honest warnings.

**Light roasts can taste sour if you brew them badly.** That's not the coffee being pretentious, it's under-extraction — you didn't pull enough out of the grounds. It's fixable and the [brewing variables](#) section covers how.

**You may not like it immediately.** If you've drunk dark, sweetened coffee your whole life, a bright washed Ethiopian is going to read as "fruit juice, not coffee." That's a real reaction and it doesn't make you wrong. Start in the middle — a medium roast Indian washed coffee — and work outward from there. The taste quiz in this app exists exactly so you don't start with something you'll hate.

## India is a coffee origin, not just a coffee market

Worth saying plainly, because Indian roasters often lead with imported Ethiopians as though local coffee were the compromise: India grows genuinely good coffee, mostly in Karnataka, Kerala, and Tamil Nadu. Chikmagalur, Coorg, and the Biligiris produce washed arabicas with chocolate and citrus character that hold up against anything at their price. And monsooned Malabar — beans deliberately exposed to monsoon winds until they swell and lose acidity — is a processing style that exists nowhere else on earth.

Buying Indian also means buying fresher. A bag roasted in Delhi last week beats a bag that spent two months in transit, whatever the origin on the label.
