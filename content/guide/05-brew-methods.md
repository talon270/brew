# Brewing it: five recipes that work

Each of these is a starting recipe, not a rulebook. Brew it, taste it, then use the [troubleshooting](#) section to adjust.

## French press

The most forgiving method, and the one to start with. Hard to get badly wrong, and it needs no special kit.

**30g coffee, 500ml water, coarse grind, 4 minutes.**

1. Coarse grind, into the empty press.
2. Pour all the water in at once. Start a timer.
3. At 4 minutes, stir the crust on top and skim off the foam and floating grounds.
4. Press the plunger down slowly — just past the surface, don't ram it to the bottom.
5. **Pour it all out immediately.** Coffee left sitting on the grounds keeps extracting and turns bitter.

Gives a heavy, full body. Slightly muddy by design — that's the metal filter letting oils and fines through, and it's the point.

## Pourover (V60)

The method that shows off a good light roast. More demanding, more rewarding, and where a gooseneck kettle starts to matter.

**15g coffee, 250ml water, medium grind, ~3 minutes.**

1. Put the filter in, rinse it with hot water, discard that water. This washes out papery taste and preheats everything.
2. Coffee in, level the bed, put it on the scale, tare.
3. **Bloom:** pour 45ml (3× the coffee weight), just enough to wet everything. Wait 30–45 seconds. It'll bubble and swell — that's trapped CO₂ escaping, and letting it out now stops it repelling water later.
4. Pour in slow circles up to 150ml. Pause a moment.
5. Pour up to 250ml.
6. Should finish draining around 3:00.

Draining much faster than 2:30? Grind finer. Stalling past 4:00? Grind coarser.

## AeroPress

Fast, nearly unbreakable, and the best travel method there is. Enormously flexible — the recipe below is just one of many.

**15g coffee, 230ml water, medium-fine grind, ~2 minutes.**

1. Assemble it the standard way, filter rinsed, on top of a sturdy mug.
2. Coffee in, pour water to the top, stir gently 3 times.
3. Cap on, wait 1 minute 30.
4. Press down steadily over about 30 seconds. Stop the moment you hear hissing.

Clean cup, low bitterness, very tolerant of imprecise grinding — which makes it a good match for a modest grinder.

## Moka pot

The stovetop classic. Not espresso, whatever the box says, but a genuinely good strong concentrated coffee — and excellent with milk.

**Fine grind (not espresso-fine), water to just below the valve.**

1. Fill the bottom chamber with **hot** water, up to just under the safety valve. Starting hot means less time on the heat and far less of the scorched taste moka pots get blamed for.
2. Fill the basket with fine coffee. Level it. **Do not tamp** — moka pots aren't built for that pressure.
3. Assemble, medium-low heat, lid open.
4. When coffee starts flowing out honey-coloured and steady, you're going well. When it turns pale, blond, and starts sputtering, **take it off the heat immediately** and run the base under a cold tap.

That last step is the whole game. Everything bitter about moka coffee comes from letting it run too long.

## South Indian filter

Not a lesser method — a different one, and worth taking seriously. It produces a concentrated decoction that's built for milk in a way no pourover is.

**Fine-medium grind, traditionally 70–80% coffee to 20–30% chicory.**

1. Fill the upper chamber with coffee. Tamp lightly with the press disc.
2. Pour boiling water in, cover, and leave it alone.
3. **Wait 15–20 minutes.** The decoction drips through slowly. Rushing it is the only way to fail.
4. Mix roughly 1 part decoction to 3–4 parts hot milk, sweeten to taste.
5. Pull it between two tumblers to froth and cool it.

Chicory isn't an adulterant here, it's a design choice: it adds body and a dark bittersweet depth that survives milk and sugar. A decoction of 100% light-roast arabica in this brewer will just taste weak.
