# Brewing in Indian conditions

Almost every coffee guide you will read was written in a temperate country, and quietly assumes a temperate kitchen. Some of that advice does not survive a Delhi summer or a Mumbai monsoon.

Here is what actually changes.

## Humidity moves your grind

Coffee is hygroscopic — it pulls moisture out of the air. During the monsoon, beans sitting in a humid kitchen absorb enough water to change how they break apart in the grinder.

The practical effect is that **the same grinder setting produces a finer, clumpier grind in July than it did in April**. Your brews get slower and more bitter without you touching anything.

The fix is not complicated: expect to grind slightly coarser in humid months, and re-check your setting whenever the weather turns rather than assuming your notes from last season still hold. This is a genuinely good reason to keep a brew log — it turns "my coffee is worse lately" into "my brew time went from 3:00 to 3:40 across two weeks".

Static is the opposite problem in dry winter air: grounds cling to everything and scatter. A single drop of water stirred into the beans before grinding — the "Ross Droplet Technique" — kills it almost entirely.

## Heat is the enemy of storage

The standard advice is to keep coffee somewhere cool, dark and airtight. In a Delhi summer, "cool" is doing a lot of work in that sentence.

Staling is a chemical process, and chemical processes run faster when hot. A bag that would last a month in a European kitchen fades noticeably quicker in a room that sits at 35°C. If your kitchen is hot, the practical answers are:

- **Buy smaller bags more often.** 250g you finish in two weeks beats a kilo you fight with for two months.
- **Keep the bag away from the hob.** The cupboard above the stove is the worst place in the kitchen and the most common place people store coffee.
- **Do not refrigerate.** Every time you take the bag out, condensation forms on cold beans. Moisture is exactly what you were trying to avoid.

Freezing genuinely does work, but only if you are disciplined: portion into airtight amounts you will use in one go, and never refreeze. Half-measures here are worse than just leaving it in the cupboard.

## Water is harder than the recipe assumes

This deserves its own page, and has one — but the short version belongs here too.

Delhi NCR tap water is hard and highly alkaline. Alkalinity buffers acidity, which is a polite way of saying it removes exactly the flavours you paid extra for. Brew a bright Ethiopian coffee with Delhi tap water and it will taste like a duller, more expensive version of what you were drinking before.

The usual household answer, an RO purifier, overshoots: it strips out nearly all the minerals, and minerals are what physically pull flavour out of the grounds. Water that is too empty extracts poorly and tastes hollow.

The right answer is to start from RO water and add a measured amount back. It takes ten minutes once, and lasts months.

## Filter coffee is not a lesser thing

One more assumption worth naming: guides written abroad treat South Indian filter coffee as a curiosity, if they mention it at all.

It is a genuinely distinct brewing method with its own logic — a very fine grind, a long slow drip, a high coffee-to-water ratio, and a chicory blend that exists for a reason rather than as adulteration. Judged as pourover it makes no sense. Judged on its own terms it is excellent, and the equipment costs a few hundred rupees.

If you grew up with it, you already own a brewing device that most enthusiasts abroad would have to import.
